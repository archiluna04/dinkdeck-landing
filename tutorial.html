Before you start

Dink Deck runs open play. Players find a night and join it; the host opens the desk and the people who signed up are the roster; the board deals every rotation so the wait is fair and the games are close. Everybody’s rank follows them to any court.

You need a phone for the first three chapters and, if you are going to run a night, something wider — a tablet or a laptop — for the desk. Nothing needs installing and none of it costs anything — for players and for clubs alike.

Start here

Everybody needs an account, whichever of the two you are. Five screens, asked once, and then the app knows enough to be useful.

Chapter 1

Getting in

An account, the one question that decides how your games are scored, and a look at where everything lives.

Make an account

  1. 01 /signin

    Pick a way in

    Dink Deck opens here. An email and a password works everywhere, and is the one to choose if you might run a night from a laptop later — you will sign in there too. Google is offered alongside it: one tap, no password to remember.

  2. 02 /register

    A name, an email, a password

    Your name is what the board calls you all night, so write it the way people at your court say it. Passwords need eight characters — a short sentence beats a short word with a number stuck on the end. Gender is optional, used only for mixed doubles and divisions, and never shown on your profile.

  3. 03 /welcome

    Say what level you play at

    The one question that changes how your first games are scored, and it is asked once. Work it out for me watches ten games and reveals your rank when it settles. I know my level starts you where you say — your first games still count double while it settles.

    Neither answer is a claim anybody has to take your word for. Ten games in, the ladder has its own opinion and that is the one that sticks.

  4. 04 /welcome

    The ladder, in bands you already know

    Seven ranks that line up with the DUPR and UTPR bands. Each rank holds five pips and each pip is worth 0.10, so an Intermediate 1 and an Intermediate 5 are genuinely different players and the board can tell them apart when it comes to balance a game.

  5. 05 /welcome

    Where you play

    Province and town, from the national register — all 81 provinces and 1,634 towns. This is what puts you on the boards near you and what makes Nearby work. Nothing is ever refused for want of it: skip it and add it later from your profile.

Where everything is

  1. 06 /

    Home is your feed

    What your clubs are posting, open play near you, and every night you have played with what it was worth. On day one it is mostly other people’s news.

  2. 07 /groups

    Groups is clubs and courts

    Clubs you can follow, so their nights reach your feed, and the courts near you with who plays there. Courts are a tab of this screen rather than a destination of their own.

  3. 08 /me

    You is your standing

    Rank and pips, your record, the nights you have played, your notifications and security — and, when you have one, the club you run. Your standing belongs to your account rather than to one club’s spreadsheet, so it follows you to any court in the country.

For players

You want a game. This is finding one, getting on the list, what the night looks like from the side of the court, and what you have to show for it in the morning.

Chapter 1

Finding a night

Open play near you, the page you land on when somebody sends you a link, and everything on it worth knowing before you turn up.

What is on

  1. 01 /play

    Play is what is on

    Every session laid out on a timeline. Filter to Today, Week, the Clubs you follow, or Nearby when you are away from home. Join takes a place; a full night offers Join the waitlist instead, and you move up when somebody drops out.

    Nearby is a box drawn around a point, so it only finds nights whose host dropped a pin. An address on its own is not a location.

The night’s own page

  1. 02 /events/:id

    The link opens the night

    Where it is on a map, when it starts, how many places are left, what it costs, and who is already going. No account is needed to read it — one is needed to get on the list.

  2. 03 /events/:id

    Asked in, not walked in

    Sessions are vetted by default, so the button says Request to join and your side ends at “requested” until the host says yes. A night the host does not vet says Join this session instead and puts you straight on the roster.

    Two people with the same name on one night are asked for a name for tonight, so the board never has two indistinguishable Noels.

  3. 04 /events/:id

    Who is going

    Names and ranks, so you know what kind of night it will be before you turn up. Requests and the waitlist are counted separately from the confirmed, so “10 of 12” means ten people are actually coming.

  4. 05 /events/:id

    Bringing a friend

    They take a place and play like anybody else. Say roughly how they play when you add them — a guest with no level is a stranger the matcher has to guess at, and guessing is how somebody ends up in a game nobody enjoys.

  5. 06 /events/:id

    Everybody on the night, in one thread

    Where the host says the courts are wet and somebody asks for a lift. It belongs to the night rather than to the club, so it goes quiet when the night is over instead of becoming another group chat nobody can leave.

Chapter 2

On the night

You have turned up. From here the desk is running the queue — this is your window on it.

  1. 07 /events/:id

    The live board

    Tap I’m here when you arrive: signing up last week and standing on the court tonight are different claims, and the desk only calls names that are in the queue. After that the same tab shows the courts, who is on them, the waiting list in order, and — pinned underneath — your own place in it, your rank as it stands right now, and the games you have played tonight.

    It is a window and nothing more. Every control the desk has lives behind a check the server does, so there is no way to nudge the queue from here. If something needs fixing, tell the host.

Chapter 3

After the night

What you played, what it was worth, something to post, and where it puts you on the boards near you.

Your recap

  1. 08 /recap/:sessionId

    Your night

    Your record game by game, the time actually spent on court, your best partner, and everybody you played with the head-to-head against each.

    Time on court is summed from the games you were in, not from when the doors opened. Three hours at the venue of which you played forty minutes is not three hours of pickleball.

  2. 09 /recap/:sessionId

    Something to post

    Pick a card — a win streak, the rank you climbed to, the night’s figures — and the one button under the rail acts on it. On a phone it opens your share sheet, which is how it reaches Facebook, Messenger or Instagram, and how Save Image gets it into Photos. On a laptop there is no sheet, so it downloads instead.

    The cards are drawn transparent at story size, so they sit on whatever background the app you post them to gives them.

  3. 10 /recap/:sessionId

    Say what was good

    A rating knows who won. It does not know who made the walk-up welcome, or kept their head when the score got away, or hit the one ATP everybody is still talking about. Commends are the other half — what the room says, rather than what the scoreboard says.

    Positive only, and there is no downvote coming. A negative counterpart would turn the one thing you may write about somebody else into a weapon.

  4. 11 /recap/:sessionId

    Who made your night

    Everybody you actually shared a court with, and whoever ran the night. You cannot commend somebody you never played: the server checks that, not just the screen.

  5. 12 /recap/:sessionId

    And what they did

    Shots, the way they played, the way they were to play with. Pick as many as fit — there is a cap on how many people one person can name, and it is the server’s number, so the chips stop at exactly the point the API would refuse.

  6. 13 /recap/:sessionId

    The whole night, not just yours

    Top of the board, the highlights, and every game in the order it was played. A host can flip between players with the chips at the top; everybody else reads their own.

What it did to your standing

  1. 14 /

    On your front page

    The night lands on Home with three figures from three different places: the record from the games, the rating movement banked at the moment the host closed the session, and the time from the game clocks themselves.

    The movement is the one that cannot be worked out afterwards — a rating is a running total, so if the close does not bank what it was before, nothing later can recover it.

  2. 15 /me

    Your standing

    Rank and pips, and your record all time. Win a game and you gain a pip; lose one and you drop a pip. The size of the step depends on who you beat, so beating a stronger side is worth more than beating a weaker one.

  3. 16 /me

    Every night you have played

    Each one keeps its own record and what it was worth, and links back to its recap. Nothing is lost when a club stops running.

The boards

  1. 17 /stats

    Your statistics

    Reached from Your statistics on your profile. Games by day and by month, the venues you play most, and the line your rating has taken night by night.

  2. 18 /stats

    Who is climbing, everywhere

    The boards rank on rating gained since the board last settled, so a good run shows up here long before it moves a standing anywhere else. Your own place is pinned at the top whatever it is — finding yourself is most of the reason anybody opens a leaderboard.

  3. 19 /stats

    Your province

    The same board narrowed to your province. This is what the town and province questions at sign-up were for.

  4. 20 /stats

    Your town

    Narrowed again. A town board is small enough to actually be somebody on, which is the point of having one.

  5. 21 /stats

    All time, or the last seven days

    The board settles once a week, so a placing holds for the week instead of shifting after every game. The arrow beside a name is places moved since it last settled.

    You appear on a board once you are past your ten placement games and your level has settled.

  6. 22 /stats

    The board that is not a ladder

    The other half of the commends. Ranked on how many different people said something good about you rather than on how much you win — and a tag of its own answers “who is the best dinker here”. Nothing from the ladder leaks onto it: no rank badge, no rating.

  7. 23 /medals

    The whole ladder

    Thirty steps end to end, Beginner 1 to Pro 5, and where you are on it. Crossing a rank raises its own notice at the desk, so a promotion is something the room sees.

For hosts

You want to run a night. Anyone can — there is no application and no invite code. This is a club, a session on the calendar, a roster, and the desk that deals the whole evening.

Chapter 1

Your club, and its first night

A club is one screen. Posting a night is three steps. Together they take about two minutes.

Start a club

  1. 01 /o/new

    Anyone can host

    From You, tap create one under “Run your own open play”. A club or group is a set of people; a court or venue is a place that runs its own nights. There is no application and nothing to be approved for.

  2. 02 /o/:slug

    Your club page

    Sessions, a feed you can post to, members and payments all live here. It is also the page you share when somebody asks where you play, and it reads perfectly well for people with no account.

Post a night

  1. 03 /o/:slug/events/new

    What and where

    Name the night, say where it is, and set the date and time — or take one of the presets. Then tap Pin it. The address is for reading; the pin is what a search “near me” actually answers, and a night with no pin is outside every one of those boxes.

    The pin uses the phone’s own location, so drop it while you are at the venue — or fix it later from the edit screen.

  2. 04 /o/:slug/events/new

    How the night runs

    How many courts, and a cap on numbers. Two courts seat eight at a time and everybody else waits their turn, so a cap of twelve on two courts is a busy but rotating night. Sixteen on two is a lot of sitting.

  3. 05 /o/:slug/events/new

    Read it back, then post

    The last step shows the night as a player will see it. Posting publishes it: it is on the Play timeline, it is in the feed of everyone following the club, and it has a link you can send.

  4. 06 /events/:id

    It is live

    The night’s own page, and the one you will come back to all week. Put a photo on it, message the people who are in, and — when the evening comes — open the desk from here.

  5. 07 /events/:id

    Playing, or running it?

    Only whoever runs the club is asked this. Playing tonight takes a place and puts you in the queue like anybody else — you can still run the board. Running it, not playing keeps your place free for a player.

  6. 08 /events/:id

    Getting it in front of people

    A link anybody can open, whether or not they have an account. It also lands on the Play timeline and in the feed of everyone following the club, and people nearby are told a new night has been posted.

  7. 09 /events/:id/edit

    Nothing is final

    Courts, numbers, the time, the fee, whether you vet who comes — all of it changes after posting, and everybody already in is told. Cancelling is here too, and it is a status rather than a deletion: the night stays readable for the people who had planned around it.

Chapter 2

Filling it

Requests come in all week. This is the half hour before the first game, and it is mostly saying yes.

  1. 10 /events/:id

    Confirm who asked in

    Every request with the rank they play at and, if they have played your nights before, how often they did not turn up. Confirm the ones you want; the rest stay requested or go on the waitlist, and nobody is left guessing which.

  2. 11 /events/:id

    The roster is the night

    Nobody types a name at the desk. The people who signed up are the people the board deals, and their standing comes with them — which is what makes the first game of the evening a fair one instead of a guess.

    A regular with no account is added here too. A walk-up takes a place and plays like anybody else; they simply carry no standing between nights.

Chapter 3

Running the desk

It deals every rotation, keeps the wait fair, and does all of it on the device in front of you — a court with dead wifi keeps rotating.

Opening the night

  1. 12 /signin

    Sign in where you will run it

    A laptop or a tablet at the desk, on the same account. Anything wider than a phone gets the two-column console; a phone gets a one-handed version of the same board rather than a squeezed copy of the other.

  2. 13 /host/:eventId

    Open the desk

    View session on the event page opens the console — organizers only; the server refuses anybody else rather than hiding the link and hoping. It shows who is expected and then waits: opening the night is a deliberate act, not something that happens because somebody looked at the page.

  3. 14 /host/:eventId

    Check people in as they arrive

    The night begins with an empty room. Nobody is dealt into a game until they are checked in — tap them one at a time as they walk up, or Check all in when the room is already full.

The board

  1. 15 /host/:eventId

    What you are looking at

    Courts on one side, the queue on the other. Games are planned together rather than picked one at a time, so the board seats as many people as it can and match quality only breaks ties — which is what stops an awkward-to-pair player sitting out all night while everyone around them plays.

    Whoever has played fewest games is called first, always. Nobody plays their sixth while somebody waits on their third.

  2. 16 /host/:eventId

    Who is on, and who is next

    Each court shows the match it will actually play — Team A over Team B, with the projected rating on each side. The waiting list is split the same way: On next, Then, Still waiting, each headed with the matchup and who is partnered with whom.

  3. 17 /host/:eventId

    Everything the desk can do

    One row along the top, and it is the whole console. Fill all courts seats every open one at once. Then, in order: Players, Rank medals, Session results, Session setup, How this works, and Finish. Every one of them is named and explained in Every control on the desk, in the reference.

  4. 18 /host/:eventId

    And everything you can do to one player

    Three controls on every row. Priority stars them to the front — for a coach, a member, or somebody who has to leave soon. Partner pins two people together for the night. Sit out takes them off the queue until they tap back in. A fourth, Skip, appears only on somebody who has just been called: nobody further back has a game to be pulled out of yet.

    Priority reorders the queue; it does not exempt anybody from the matchmaking range, so a starred Beginner still waits for a game they belong in.

  5. 19 /host/:eventId

    The room, in one sheet

    Not just the queue: everyone signed in, who is on court and which one, and who is sitting out with a one-tap way back. This is also where you add the regular who does not use the app.

  6. 20 /host/:eventId

    How the night is matched

    Setup holds the courts, the optional challenger court, and the matchmaking range — how far apart players in one game may be, counted in ladder steps, where a pip is one step and a rank is five. It works itself out from who is actually in the room, so there is nothing to set.

    If nobody waiting satisfies the range, the court says so and offers a one-off override. It never quietly starts a mismatched game, and it never deadlocks either.

Playing the games

  1. 21 /host/:eventId

    Lodge the result

    End the game, type the score, and confirm the side that won. The score names its own winner — the buttons only confirm it, which is what stops “Team A won, 7–11” being lodged by a mis-tap.

    A score the clock could not have held is recorded but flagged, and its margin counts for nobody’s rank. Eleven points in four minutes is a typo, or a court that never really played it.

  2. 22 /host/:eventId

    Recorded, and on its way

    The next four go straight on. Results queue on the device and send when there is signal — the counter tells you how many are still to go, and a replayed batch is a no-op rather than a game counted twice.

    The desk never depends on the network. If the wifi dies mid-session the queue keeps rotating and everything arrives when it comes back.

  3. 23 /host/:eventId

    Tonight, so far

    Session results is the night’s own leaderboard and its history — who has won what, and every game in the order it was played.

  4. 24 /host/:eventId

    Re-draw the order

    The night opened in check-in order, which is the order people happened to arrive in. One tap re-draws it — seeded, so it survives a re-render and a reload without anybody appearing to jump the line.

  5. 25 /host/:eventId

    Or choose the four yourself

    For the game the queue will not offer — a coach who wants a particular pairing, or four friends who came together and want one game before the night swallows them. The board picks up again afterwards.

Chapter 4

Closing out

The step that actually writes the night back to everybody who played it — and what is left afterwards.

  1. 26 /host/:eventId

    Last game in

    Wait until the counter says nothing is left to send. Everything on this screen is already on the device; this is about the server having it too.

  2. 27 /host/:eventId

    Finish and post the results

    This is what writes the night back to everybody who played it: their record, their pips, and what their rating was before and after. It is the only moment that can bank the “before”, which is why the app asks rather than assuming.

  3. 28 /host/:eventId

    The desk is shut

    Coming back to a closed night gets this instead of a board. If somebody turns up late and you want another hour, Reopen the night picks the board up exactly where it was closed — nothing that was banked is lost.

  4. 29 /recap/:sessionId

    The club’s post of the night

    The whole session as a card — the board, the record, and the club’s own mark ghosted into the ground, in the club’s colours if it has them. This is the one you put on the club page or in the group chat the morning after. Host-only, because posting it is the club speaking.

  5. 30 /o/:slug/payments

    What the night took

    Who has paid and who has not, night by night. The money itself never goes through Dink Deck — a club’s fee is settled in GCash or cash on the night, and this only keeps the count so nobody has to remember it.

The ladder

Seven ranks, five pips each, thirty steps end to end. A pip is worth 0.10, so the ladder lines up exactly with the DUPR and UTPR bands players already quote — and the board can tell an Intermediate 1 from an Intermediate 5 when it comes to balance a game.

Ranks and the bands they map to
#RankRatingWho it is
1Beginner2.0 – 2.49New to the game. Learning the serve, the rules, and how to keep score.
2Novice2.5 – 2.74Can sustain a short rally. Starting to control depth and direction.
3Improver2.75 – 2.99Rallies hold up. Getting to the kitchen line and staying there.
4Intermediate3.0 – 3.49Reliable serve and return. Moving to the line with intent.
5Skilled3.5 – 3.99Consistent third-shot drops, dinks with patience, covers the stack.
6Advanced4.0 – 4.49Competitive tournament play. Controls pace and attacks the high ball.
7Pro4.5 +Open level. Wins points with speed-ups, resets and shot shaping.

Win a game and you gain a pip; lose one and you drop a pip; a game ended with no winner moves nobody. The size of the step depends on who you beat. Your first ten games are placement games and count double while your level settles.
