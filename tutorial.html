Before you start

Dink Deck runs open play. Players find a night and join it; the host opens the desk and the people who signed up are the roster; the board deals every rotation so the wait is fair and the games are close. Everybody’s rank follows them to any court.

You need a phone for the first three chapters and, if you are going to run a night, something wider — a tablet or a laptop — for the desk. Nothing needs installing and none of it costs anything — for players and for clubs alike.

Chapter 1

Getting in

An account, a look around, and — because anyone can host — a club of your own with a night on the calendar.

Make an account

  1. 01 /signin

    Pick a way in

    Dink Deck opens here. An email and a password works everywhere, and is the one to choose if you might run a night from a laptop later — you will sign in there too. Google is offered alongside it: one tap, no password to remember.

  2. 02 /register

    Three fields and a name

    Your name is what the board calls you all night, so write it the way people at your court say it. Passwords need eight characters — a short sentence beats a short word with a number stuck on the end. Gender is optional, used only for mixed doubles and divisions, and never shown on your profile.

  3. 03 /welcome

    Say what level you play at

    This is the one question that changes how your first games are scored, and it is asked once. Work it out for me watches ten games and reveals your rank when it settles. I know my level starts you where you say — your first games still count double while it settles.

  4. 04 /welcome

    The ladder, in bands you already know

    Seven ranks that line up with the DUPR and UTPR bands. Each rank holds five pips and each pip is worth 0.10, so an Intermediate 1 and an Intermediate 5 are genuinely different players and the board can tell them apart.

  5. 05 /welcome

    Where you play

    Province and town, from the national register. This is what puts you on the boards near you and what makes Nearby work when you go looking for a game. Nothing is refused for want of it — skip it and add it later from your profile.

Look around

  1. 06 /

    Home is your feed

    What your clubs are posting, open play near you, and every night you have played with what it was worth. On day one it is mostly other people’s news.

  2. 07 /play

    Play is what is on

    Every session laid out on a timeline. Filter to Today, Week, the Clubs you follow, or Nearby when you are away from home. Join takes a place; a full night offers Join the waitlist instead, and you move up when somebody drops out.

  3. 08 /groups

    Groups is clubs and courts

    Clubs you can follow so their nights reach your feed, and the courts near you with who plays there. Courts are a tab of this screen rather than a destination of their own.

  4. 09 /me

    You is your standing

    Rank and pips, your record, the nights you have played, your notifications and security. Your standing belongs to your account, not to one club’s spreadsheet, so it follows you to any court.

Start a club

  1. 10 /o/new

    Anyone can host

    From You, tap create one under “Run your own open play”. A club or group is a set of people; a court or venue is a place that runs its own nights. This is the only thing standing between you and a session.

  2. 11 /o/:slug

    Your club page

    Sessions, a feed you can post to, members and payments all live here. It is also the page you share when somebody asks where you play.

Post a night

  1. 12 /o/:slug/events/new

    What and where

    Name the night, say where it is, and set the date and time — or take one of the presets. Then tap Pin it. The address is for reading; the pin is what a search “near me” actually answers, and a night with no pin is outside every one of those boxes.

    The pin uses your phone’s location, so drop it while you are at the venue — or edit the night later from the courts.

  2. 13 /o/:slug/events/new

    How the night runs

    How many courts, and a cap on numbers. Two courts seat eight at a time and everybody else waits their turn, so a cap of twelve on two courts is a busy but rotating night.

  3. 14 /o/:slug/events/new

    Read it back, then post

    The last step shows the night as a player will see it. Posting publishes it: it is on the Play timeline, it is in the feed of anyone following the club, and it has a link you can send.

  4. 15 /events/:id

    It is live

    The night’s own page. Share it, put a photo on it, message the people who are in, and — when the evening comes — open the desk from here.

  5. 16 /events/:id

    Playing, or running it?

    Only whoever runs the club is asked this. Playing tonight takes a place and puts you in the queue like anybody else — you can still run the board. Running it, not playing keeps your place free for a player.

Chapter 2

Someone joins

The other side of the same page. This is what a player sees when your link reaches them, and what you have to do about it.

  1. 17 /events/:id

    The link opens the night

    Where it is on a map, when it starts, how many places are left, what it costs, and who is already going. No account is needed to read it — one is needed to get on the list.

  2. 18 /events/:id

    Asked in, not walked in

    Sessions are vetted by default, so the button says Request to join and the player’s side ends at “requested”. A night you do not vet says Join this session instead and puts them straight on the roster.

    Two people with the same name on one night are asked for a name for tonight, so the board never has two indistinguishable Noels.

  3. 19 /events/:id

    Who is going

    Names and ranks, so a player knows what kind of night it will be before they turn up. Requests and the waitlist are counted separately from the confirmed.

Chapter 3

Running the night

The desk. It deals every rotation, keeps the wait fair, and does all of it on the device in front of you — a court with dead wifi keeps rotating.

Before the first game

  1. 20 /signin

    Sign in where you will run it

    A laptop or a tablet at the desk, on the same account. Anything wider than a phone gets the two-column console; a phone gets a one-handed version of the same board.

  2. 21 /events/:id

    Confirm who asked in

    Every request with the rank they play at. Confirm the ones you want; the rest stay requested or go on the waitlist. This is also where you add a regular who does not use the app.

  3. 22 /events/:id

    The roster is the night

    Nobody types a name at the desk. The people who signed up are the people the board deals, and their standing comes with them.

  4. 23 /host/:eventId

    Open the desk

    View session on the event page opens the console — organizers only; the server refuses anybody else. It shows who is expected and waits. Opening the night is a deliberate act, not something that happens because somebody looked at the page.

  5. 24 /host/:eventId

    Check people in as they arrive

    Signing up last week and standing on the court tonight are different claims. Nobody is dealt into a game until they are checked in — tap them individually, or Check all in when the room is full.

The rotation

  1. 25 /host/:eventId

    The board

    Courts on one side, the queue on the other. Games are planned together rather than picked one at a time, so the board seats as many people as it can and match quality only breaks ties — which is what stops an awkward-to-pair player sitting out all night.

  2. 26 /host/:eventId

    Who is on, and who is next

    Each court shows the match it will actually play — Team A over Team B, with the projected rating on each side. The waiting list is split the same way: On next, Then, Still waiting, each headed with the matchup and who is partnered with whom.

  3. 27 /host/:eventId

    Everything the desk can do

    One row along the top, and it is the whole console. Fill all courts seats every open one at once. Then, in order: Players, Rank medals, Session results, Session setup, How this works, and Finish. Every one of them is named and explained in Every control on the desk, at the end of this guide.

  4. 28 /host/:eventId

    And everything you can do to one player

    Three controls on every row. Priority stars them to the front — for a coach, a member, or somebody who has to leave soon. Partner pins two people together for the night. Sit out takes them off the queue until they tap back in. A fourth, Skip, appears only on somebody who has just been called: nobody further back has a game to be pulled out of yet.

    Priority reorders the queue; it does not exempt anybody from the matchmaking range, so a starred Beginner still waits for a game they belong in.

  5. 29 /host/:eventId

    The room, in one sheet

    Not just the queue: everyone signed in, who is on court and which one, and who is sitting out with a one-tap way back. This is also where you add the regular who does not use the app — a walk-up takes a place and plays, and simply carries no standing between nights.

  6. 30 /host/:eventId

    How the night is matched

    Setup holds the courts, the optional challenger court, and the matchmaking range — how far apart players in one game may be, counted in ladder steps, where a pip is one step and a rank is five. It works itself out from who is actually in the room. If nobody waiting satisfies it, the court says so and offers a one-off override; it never quietly starts a mismatched game.

  7. 31 /host/:eventId

    Lodge the result

    End the game, type the score, and confirm the side that won. The score names its own winner — the buttons only confirm it, which is what stops “Team A won, 7–11” being lodged by a mis-tap. A score the clock could not have held is recorded but flagged, and its margin counts for nobody’s rank.

  8. 32 /host/:eventId

    Recorded, and on its way

    The next four go straight on. Results queue on the device and send when there is signal — the counter tells you how many are still to go, and a replayed batch is a no-op rather than a game counted twice.

    The desk never depends on the network. If the wifi dies mid-session the queue keeps rotating and everything arrives when it comes back.

  9. 33 /host/:eventId

    Tonight, so far

    Session results is the night’s own leaderboard and its history — who has won what, and every game in the order it was played.

Closing out

  1. 34 /host/:eventId

    Last game in

    Wait until the counter says nothing is left to send. Everything on this screen is already on the device; this is about the server having it too.

  2. 35 /host/:eventId

    Finish and post the results

    This is what writes the night back to everybody who played it: their record, their pips, and what their rating was before and after. It is the only moment that can bank the “before”, which is why the app asks rather than assuming.

  3. 36 /host/:eventId

    The desk is shut

    Coming back to a closed night gets this instead of a board. If somebody turns up late and you want another hour, Resume reopens it.

Chapter 4

The morning after

What the night was worth — to you, to everybody who was there, and to the boards you are now on.

The recap

  1. 37 /recap/:sessionId

    Your night

    Your record game by game, time actually spent on court, your best partner, and everybody you played with the head-to-head against each. Underneath it, share cards sized for a story — a win streak, a rank badge, the night’s figures.

  2. 38 /recap/:sessionId

    Something to post

    Pick a card — a win streak, the rank you climbed to, the night’s figures — and the one button under the rail acts on it. On a phone it opens your share sheet, which is how it reaches Facebook, Messenger or Instagram, and how Save Image gets it into Photos. On a laptop there is no sheet, so it downloads instead.

    The cards are drawn transparent at story size, so they sit on whatever background the app you post them to gives them.

  3. 39 /recap/:sessionId

    The whole night

    Top of the board, the highlights, and every game in order. A host can flip between players with the chips at the top; everybody else reads their own.

  4. 40 /recap/:sessionId

    And the night, for the club

    The same again for the session rather than the person — the board, the record, and the club’s own mark ghosted into the ground. This is the one a host posts on the club page or to the group chat the morning after.

What it did to your standing

  1. 41 /

    On your front page

    The night lands on Home with three figures from three different places: the record from the games, the rating movement banked when the host closed the session, and the time summed from the games you were actually in — not from when the doors opened.

  2. 42 /me

    Your standing

    Rank and pips, your record, and the clubs you help run. A win is a pip up and a loss is a pip down, with the size of the step depending on who you beat and how the game went.

  3. 43 /me

    Every night you have played

    Each one keeps its own record and what it was worth, and links back to its recap.

The boards

  1. 44 /stats

    Your statistics

    Reached from Your statistics on your profile. Games by day and by month, the venues you play most, and the line your rating has taken night by night.

  2. 45 /stats

    Who is climbing, everywhere

    The boards rank on rating gained since the board last settled, so a good run shows up here long before it moves a standing anywhere else. Your own place is pinned at the top whatever it is — finding yourself is most of the reason anybody opens a leaderboard.

  3. 46 /stats

    Your province

    The same board narrowed to Pampanga. This is what the town and province questions at sign-up were for.

  4. 47 /stats

    Your town

    Narrowed again. A town board is small enough to actually be somebody on, which is the point of having one.

  5. 48 /stats

    All time, or the last seven days

    The board settles once a week, so a placing holds for the week instead of shifting after every game. The arrow beside a name is places moved since it last settled.

    You appear on a board once you are past your placement games — ten of them — and your level is settled.

  6. 49 /medals

    The whole ladder

    Thirty steps end to end, Beginner 1 to Pro 5, and where you are on it. Crossing a rank raises its own notice at the desk, so a promotion is something the room sees.

The ladder

Seven ranks, five pips each, thirty steps end to end. A pip is worth 0.10, so the ladder lines up exactly with the DUPR and UTPR bands players already quote — and the board can tell an Intermediate 1 from an Intermediate 5 when it comes to balance a game.

Ranks and the bands they map to
#RankRatingWho it is
1Beginner2.0 – 2.49New to the game. Learning the serve, the rules, and how to keep score.
2Novice2.5 – 2.74Can sustain a short rally. Starting to control depth and direction.
3Improver2.75 – 2.99Rallies hold up. Getting to the kitchen line and staying there.
4Intermediate3.0 – 3.49Reliable serve and return. Moving to the line with intent.
5Skilled3.5 – 3.99Consistent third-shot drops, dinks with patience, covers the stack.
6Advanced4.0 – 4.49Competitive tournament play. Controls pace and attacks the high ball.
7Pro4.5 +Open level. Wins points with speed-ups, resets and shot shaping.

Win a game and you gain a pip; lose one and you drop a pip; a game ended with no winner moves nobody. The size of the step depends on who you beat. Your first ten games are placement games and count double while your level settles.
